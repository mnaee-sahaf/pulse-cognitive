# Pulse cognitive training app: scientific evidence base

**Pulse's four dimensions — Reaction Speed, Working Memory, Cognitive Flexibility, and Decision Speed — are all measurable and improvable through practice, but their transfer profiles differ dramatically.** Processing speed training (via adaptive UFOV-type paradigms) holds the strongest evidence for real-world transfer, including a landmark 25% dementia risk reduction over 20 years in the ACTIVE trial. Working memory training, by contrast, shows essentially null far transfer despite robust near-transfer gains. Cognitive flexibility and decision speed fall between these poles, with emerging but limited generalization evidence. The sections below synthesize findings from over 100 papers — predominantly meta-analyses and systematic reviews from 2020–2026 — to inform Pulse's scientific positioning, adaptive engine design, gap analysis, claims framework, and reference library.

---

## SECTION 1: Reaction speed is a robust biomarker with strong training evidence

### Transfer is mostly near, with one critical exception

RT training transfer is **predominantly task-specific**. Gobet & Sala (2023), in a second-order meta-analysis across all cognitive training types, found that "the overall effect of far transfer is null." Basak, Qin, & O'Connell (2020), analyzing 215 RCTs, reported processing speed training yielded significant near transfer (**g = 0.37**) but significantly lower far transfer than executive functions training. A 2024 meta-analysis in *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* found WM-related improvements jumped from **SMD = 0.18** to **SMD = 1.15** when assessment tasks resembled training tasks, underscoring the task-similarity problem.

The critical exception is the **ACTIVE trial's speed-of-processing training** (adaptive UFOV paradigm), which produced functional real-world benefits — likely because it engages divided attention, stimulus discrimination, and implicit learning simultaneously rather than isolated RT.

### Durability is exceptional for adaptive speed training

The ACTIVE trial provides the strongest retention evidence in all of cognitive training. Rebok et al. (2014) showed speed training maintained cognitive improvements at **10 years** (ES = 0.66, 99% CI = 0.43–0.88), with 70.7% of speed-trained participants performing at or above baseline. Ball et al. (2013) found a single booster session counteracted **4.92 months** of age-related decline. Most remarkably, **Coe et al. (2026)** — published February 2026 in *Alzheimer's & Dementia: TRCI* — demonstrated that boosted speed training produced a **25% lower dementia diagnosis rate** over 20 years (HR = 0.75). This is the first RCT showing any intervention reducing dementia risk over two decades.

### Adaptive choice RT outperforms simple RT

The evidence hierarchy for paradigms is clear: **adaptive speed-of-processing (UFOV-type) > choice RT > simple RT**. Choice RT engages prefrontal decision-making, response selection, and inhibition — it correlates more strongly with intelligence (r = −0.44 to −0.53) than simple RT (r = −0.27 to −0.32) per Der & Deary (2017). Hardwick et al. (2022) found age-related RT increases result from slower motor *preparation*, not delayed initiation, suggesting training preparation processes is the productive intervention target. For Pulse, adaptive choice RT with progressive tempo compression and divided attention components is the most evidence-supported paradigm.

### RT predicts mortality, cognitive decline, and intelligence

RT is among the strongest cognitive biomarkers available. Deary & Der (2005) showed RT fully mediated the IQ-mortality association. Hagger-Johnson et al. (2014), using NHANES-III data (N = 5,134), found 1 SD slower RT → **HR 1.25 for all-cause mortality** and **HR 1.36 for CVD mortality** — magnitudes comparable to smoking. RT variability (intraindividual variability/IIV) is particularly informative: Jutten et al. (2023) showed IIV predicts cognitive decline even in currently unimpaired adults. Talboom et al. (2021), with over 75,666 participants, confirmed visual RT is "a valuable index of age-related cognitive decline." The RT-intelligence correlation across 172 studies averages **r = −0.24** (Sheppard & Vernon, 2008), strengthening with task complexity up to r = −0.53 for choice RT (Der & Deary, 2017).

## SECTION 2: Working memory training produces near but not far transfer

### The field has largely resolved against fluid intelligence transfer

The WM training → fluid intelligence debate, ignited by Jaeggi et al. (2008), has moved toward resolution. The weight of evidence now strongly favors skeptics:

- **Melby-Lervåg, Redick, & Hulme (2016)**: 87 publications, 145 comparisons — far-transfer effects close to zero (g ≈ 0.00–0.08) with active controls. Mediation analyses showed WM improvement was NOT related to far-transfer magnitude.
- **Sala & Gobet (2019, 2020)**: Second-order meta-analyses found far transfer "close to zero" across all populations (children, adults, older adults) and all training types. In typically developing children: **ḡ = 0.001** (SE = 0.055) for far transfer with active controls.
- **Watrin, Hülür, & Wilhelm (2024)**: 52 independent comparisons found small WM improvement (SMD = 0.18) that inflated to **SMD = 1.15** when assessment tasks were similar to training tasks — strongly suggesting practice effects, not genuine capacity increase.
- **Gobet & Sala (2023)**: Concluded far transfer "is null" with placebo effects of ~0.15–0.20 SMD always present. Called the field "in search of a phenomenon."

A minority position persists. **Pahor et al. (2022)** in *Nature Human Behaviour* demonstrated across 3 RCTs (N = 460) that near transfer to untrained n-back tasks *mediates* far transfer to Matrix Reasoning — a "gating model" where only individuals who show near transfer exhibit far transfer. **Li, Liu, & Chen (2026)** in *npj Digital Medicine* found moderate behavioral benefit (Hedges' g = 0.503) with neuroimaging evidence of neural efficiency gains. But these findings do not overturn the dominant pattern.

### Spatial training shows slightly stronger transfer than verbal

Multiple lines of evidence favor spatial/visuospatial WM training. Melby-Lervåg & Hulme (2013) found visuospatial WM gains were maintained at follow-up while verbal WM gains were not. Gathercole et al. (2019) found transfer was weaker for verbal than visuospatial material. Steyvers et al. (2019) directly compared spatial n-back to verbal complex span: only the spatial n-back group showed near-transfer evidence. Far transfer to fluid intelligence has been observed most often from **dual n-back training** (which inherently includes spatial components) rather than complex span tasks. For Pulse, spatial and dual-modality tasks should be prioritized.

### Optimal dose parameters converge around 3 sessions per week

The literature converges on specific training parameters. **Lampit et al. (2014)**, the key dose-response meta-analysis (52 RCTs, N = 4,885), found **3 sessions per week** optimal — more sessions *neutralized* efficacy. Session duration should be **15–30 minutes**; sessions under 30 minutes may be insufficient for synaptic plasticity. Total training of **15–25 sessions** over **4–8 weeks** captures most gains. Wang, Zhou, & Shah (2014) demonstrated that *spaced* sessions produced more transfer than massed sessions. Belleville et al. (2022) found a non-linear dose-response with plateau at **12–14 training hours**.

### Gains plateau after approximately 10–15 sessions

WM improvement follows a **power law learning curve** with rapid initial gains and subsequent plateau. Kuniyoshi et al. (2014) trained participants on 3-back across 21 sessions and found most gains occurred early, with individual plateau levels predictable from resting-state connectivity. Redick (2019) argued improvements reflect strategy acquisition rather than genuine capacity increase, which has inherent biological limits. Task variation is critical to avoid strategy-specific plateaus — introducing new variants maintains challenge.

### WM capacity strongly predicts real-world outcomes

Despite weak training transfer, WM capacity itself is among the strongest predictors of life outcomes. **Alloway & Alloway (2010)** showed WM is a better predictor of academic achievement than IQ. Colom et al. (2010) found WMC predicted multitasking performance in air traffic controllers beyond intelligence alone. Kyllonen & Christal (1990) established WM capacity is nearly isomorphic with general intelligence (r ≈ .80–.90 at the latent level). However, the critical caveat is that correlational importance does not validate training transfer — improving WM scores through training has not convincingly translated to improving these real-world outcomes.

## SECTION 3: Cognitive flexibility is trainable, and the mutation system is well-grounded

### The mutation system maps directly to validated paradigms

Pulse's mutation system — mid-session rule changes including mirror, reverse, and poison/inhibit rules — maps well onto the gold-standard task-switching paradigm. Miyake et al. (2000) established shifting as a distinct executive function, and Koch et al. (2018) confirmed task-switching paradigms are "relatively well understood in terms of their processing requirements." The mutation system's specific features are well-matched:

- **Rule-switching mid-session** operationalizes the task-switching paradigm
- **Mirror/reverse rules** map onto reversal learning and intradimensional shifting
- **Poison/inhibit rules** engage inhibitory control overlapping with the shifting construct
- **Unpredictable changes** create both mixing costs (maintaining awareness that rules might change) and local switch costs (adapting when they do)

**Egner & Siqi-Liu (2024)** in *Current Opinion in Behavioral Sciences* emphasized that flexibility is not fixed — people dynamically adapt their readiness to switch based on contextual demands ("meta-flexibility"), directly supporting mid-session rule changes as a valid challenge.

### Flexibility can be trained, with a breakthrough in abstract transfer

**Karbach & Kray (2009)** is the landmark training study, demonstrating that four sessions of task-switching training produced near transfer (reduced mixing and switching costs) and far transfer (improvements in inhibition, WM, and fluid intelligence) across children, young adults, and older adults. However, replication has been mixed — Pereg et al. (2013) found practice improvements but no far transfer; Zinke et al. (2012) found partial replication.

The most important recent finding is **Wen, Geddert, Madlon-Kay, & Egner (2023)** in *Psychological Science*: participants trained in high-volatility environments (frequent rule changes) acquired **abstract expectations about cognitive control demands** that transferred to completely novel task rules AND novel stimuli. This is the first demonstration of extracting and reusing cognitive control learning parameters that transcend specific tasks — directly validating Pulse's approach of training in environments with frequent rule changes.

However, **Siqi-Liu & Egner (2025)** found flexibility adaptations are **task-set specific** — they transfer to novel stimuli within the same task sets but not necessarily across different task types. This means Pulse should incorporate diverse task types rather than a single paradigm.

### Flexibility predicts creativity and real-world adaptability

Cognitive flexibility is linked to divergent thinking (Wu & Koutstaal, 2020), adaptive decision-making in experts (Laureiro-Martínez & Brusoni, 2018), academic achievement, employment success, and quality of life (Uddin, 2021). The OECD (2024) identified cognitive flexibility as "a prerequisite skill for the ability to take the perspectives of others" and essential for "thriving in an increasingly fast-paced and unpredictable" world.

### Mixing costs — not switch costs — drive age-related decline

A critical nuance from Wasylyshyn, Verhaeghen, & Sliwinski (2011): aging primarily impairs **mixing costs** (maintaining and coordinating multiple task sets simultaneously, showing an additive increase of ~146 ms) while **local switch costs** show no specific age deficit beyond general slowing. This means aging affects the sustained control component of flexibility rather than the moment-to-moment switching ability. Ferguson et al. (2021), studying 350 participants ages 10–86, found working memory and inhibitory control declined from age 30–40, while the switch cost component of flexibility "did not significantly change across the lifespan" once general slowing was controlled. Flexibility is therefore NOT the first EF to decline — WM and inhibition show earlier drops.

## SECTION 4: Decision speed is partially distinct and its threshold is trainable

### Factor-analytic and genomic evidence supports partial independence

The Cattell-Horn-Carroll (CHC) framework explicitly recognizes **Decision Speed/Reaction Time (Gt)** as a broad ability distinct from Processing Speed (Gs). **Li et al. (2024)** in *Nature Communications* used genomic structural equation modeling on GWAS data (N > 100,000) to demonstrate that cognitive processing speed and accuracy have **distinct genetic architecture, white matter correlates, and psychiatric associations**. **Navajas et al. (2021)** in *Neuron* identified "decision acuity" as a common factor across 32 decision-making measures that was distinct from IQ (discriminant validity D = 0.76) with a dissociable brain connectivity signature.

Within the drift-diffusion model (DDM) framework, SAT optimization involves two dissociable components: **drift rate** (efficiency of evidence accumulation — a stable trait-like dimension) and **boundary separation** (response caution — a strategic, flexible parameter). Weigard & Sripada (2021) showed drift rate forms a **cohesive, task-general factor** across diverse cognitive tasks, suggesting it represents a genuine individual-difference dimension partially independent of simple RT and WM.

### Training reliably shifts the speed-accuracy frontier

**Reinhartz et al. (2023)** in *Journal of Cognition* analyzed 20-session training across three speeded choice-RT tasks using DDM decomposition. Drift rate increased throughout training, boundary separation decreased, and non-decision time showed early rapid decreases. These effects appeared across different task types, suggesting some generality. **Zhang & Rowe (2014)** confirmed that training produced faster AND more accurate decisions via both drift rate increases and boundary separation decreases. Critically, speed-accuracy emphasis and learning were **dissociable mechanisms** operating at different timescales. The net effect is genuine improvement of the speed-accuracy tradeoff curve — not just movement along it.

### DDM methodology has advanced dramatically since 2020

**Fengler et al. (2021)** in *eLife* introduced Likelihood Approximation Networks (LANs) enabling fast Bayesian inference for complex sequential sampling models. The HDDM toolbox has been used in **over 400 published papers**. The successor toolbox HSSM (released ~2023–2024) uses modern probabilistic programming with GPU acceleration. **Weigard et al. (2026)** reinterpreted drift rate as primarily reflecting *efficiency* rather than *speed* of evidence accumulation, drawing an important distinction between "Speed of Evidence Accumulation" and "Efficiency of Evidence Accumulation."

### Inhibitory control warrants separate treatment

In the **Miyake & Friedman (2012)** unity/diversity framework, inhibition-specific variance collapsed into the Common EF factor — suggesting inhibition may represent the core of executive function rather than a distinct dimension. However, **Raud et al. (2020)** demonstrated Go/No-Go and Stop-Signal tasks rely on **different mechanisms**: Go/No-Go involves more automatic/bottom-up "action restraint" while SST involves controlled/top-down "action cancellation." These are functionally distinct from SAT optimization, which involves calibrating the evidence threshold rather than suppressing a prepared response. For Pulse, inhibitory control should be treated as a related but separable sub-dimension — particularly because Weigard & Sripada (2021) found drift rate outperforms traditional inhibition measures for predicting self-control.

## SECTION 5: Two dimensions should be added, two should not

### Inhibitory control — strong recommendation to add

Despite Miyake & Friedman's finding that inhibition lacks unique variance at the latent level, practical reasons for inclusion are compelling. Go/No-Go is among the most naturally gamifiable cognitive tasks, with **30–40 trials feasible in 60 seconds**. Clinical relevance is high (ADHD, addiction, obesity). Allom et al. (2015) found a meta-analytic training effect of **d = 0.378** on health behaviors. A combined Go/No-Go + Stop-Signal approach showed long-term effects (SMD = −0.95, p = 0.0002) in healthy adults. Frame as "Impulse Control" rather than claiming theoretical independence from cognitive flexibility.

### Spatial reasoning — strong recommendation to add

Spatial reasoning has the **strongest trainability evidence** of any candidate dimension. **Uttal et al. (2013)**, in a landmark meta-analysis of 217 studies, found average effect size **g = 0.47** with durable effects that transferred to untrained spatial tasks. Yang et al. (2020) found even larger effects (**g = 0.96**) in young children. Mental rotation trials are inherently brief (3–10 seconds each), making 60-second sessions well-suited. Spatial reasoning diversifies Pulse beyond executive function into broader cognitive fitness and differentiates it from competitors.

### Sustained attention — do not add

The construct is fundamentally incompatible with 60-second sessions. Vigilance requires maintaining focus *over extended time* — the vigilance decrement typically becomes significant within 15 minutes. Even the shortened PVT-B (3 minutes) showed "inadequate convergent validity" with the standard 10-minute PVT. What can be measured in 60 seconds (alertness, RT variability) overlaps substantially with Pulse's existing Reaction Speed mode.

### Episodic memory — do not add as a standard dimension

True episodic LTM requires encoding in one session and retrieval hours or days later — fundamentally incompatible with single 60-second sessions. The cross-session workaround (encode today, retrieve tomorrow) creates architectural complexity and poor UX when sessions are skipped. What fits in 60 seconds (immediate recall) overlaps with Working Memory. Consider as a future multi-session challenge mode.

### Processing speed — conditional addition

If Pulse's current Reaction Speed mode uses only simple RT, a **pattern-comparison or symbol-digit task** would capture a meaningfully distinct construct (cognitive throughput vs. sensorimotor speed). Woods et al. (2017) found cognitive processing speed decline is NOT fully explained by motor performance. If Reaction Speed already uses choice RT with multiple stimulus-response mappings, the added value diminishes.

## SECTION 6: The 85% rule is a useful heuristic, not a universal law

### Wilson et al. (2019) applies cleanly to binary classification only

The "85% rule" was derived primarily from **neural network/machine learning models** applied to binary classification tasks with Gaussian noise. Wilson et al. (2019) showed the optimal error rate for gradient-descent learning is ~15.87% (i.e., ~85% accuracy). The rule was validated against animal perceptual learning data and is biologically plausible. However, **Al-Fawakhiri, Kayani, & McDougle (2023)** found the optimal success rate for motor tasks with continuous error is approximately **70%**, substantially different from 85%. The optimal number also varies with task complexity and chance level — estimates range from **64%–83%** depending on structure.

For Pulse, the recommendation is to target **~80–85% accuracy** for binary/perceptual cognitive tasks and **~65–75%** for motor-cognitive tasks. A **3-down/1-up staircase** (converging to ~79.4%) is the most practically validated adaptive algorithm.

### Trial-by-trial adaptation is well-supported

The psychophysics literature, reviewed comprehensively by Leek (2001), supports adjusting difficulty on a trial-by-trial basis using staircase or Bayesian methods. Zhao, Lesmes, & Lu (2019) found step sizes of 10–20% work best, with approximately 30 trials sufficient for reliable threshold estimates. A 2024 Frontiers study found more responsive algorithms led to faster learning rates — algorithms requiring too many consecutive correct responses before adjusting "risk negatively affecting subject motivation."

### Individual differences are substantial but hard to quantify precisely

The Challenge Point Framework (Guadagnoli & Lee, 2004) predicts that beginners benefit from lower difficulty while advanced learners benefit from higher difficulty. Bauer, Brusso, & Orvis (2012) found personality traits (conscientiousness, openness) moderate responses to adaptive difficulty. Lodge & Kennedy (2018) described a "zone of optimal confusion" whose threshold varies markedly between individuals. The principle is well-established, but quantitative precision ("Person A should train at 82%, Person B at 88%") remains elusive.

### Adaptive vs. fixed: less clear than expected

Head-to-head comparisons yield mixed results. **Flegal et al. (2019)** found adaptive training produced better transfer and neural efficiency changes. But **von Bastian & Eschen (2016)**, in a well-controlled RCT with 130 young adults, found NO difference between adaptive, randomized, and self-selected difficulty for transfer. **Lau et al. (2025)** replicated this null in 201 children. Pedullà et al. (2016) found adaptive training superior in MS patients. The emerging picture: adaptive difficulty is clearly better than fixed-easy difficulty, but when compared to other forms of variable difficulty, the advantage of algorithmic adaptation is much less clear. The key ingredient may be **exposure to appropriate difficulty variation**, not the adaptive algorithm per se. Clinical populations show stronger adaptive advantages.

### Yerkes-Dodson is a useful heuristic, not a precise law

Teigen (1994) showed the "law" has been reinterpreted by successive generations, applying it to increasingly distant concepts. The inverted-U relationship between arousal/difficulty and performance is a reasonable simplification for design purposes — too easy leads to boredom, too hard to frustration — but the underlying mechanisms are heterogeneous and context-dependent. Diamond et al. (2007) provided neurobiological support showing glucocorticoid levels and hippocampal function follow an inverted-U, lending biological plausibility.

## SECTION 7: Processing speed training dominates the transfer hierarchy

### Transfer evidence ranked by dimension

**Processing speed training** holds the strongest evidence by a wide margin. The ACTIVE trial demonstrated large near-transfer effects (ES = 1.46 at immediate posttest per Ball et al., 2002), maintained cognitive effects at 10 years (ES = 0.66), transfer to everyday functional speed, driving maintenance (Cohen's d = 0.60 with boosters), and **25% lower dementia risk** at 20 years with boosters (Coe et al., 2026). No other training modality approaches these outcomes.

**Working memory training** has strong near transfer but essentially null far transfer. Melby-Lervåg et al. (2016) found "no convincing evidence of any reliable improvements" on far-transfer measures with active controls. The ACTIVE trial's memory training effects did not persist to 10 years (ES = 0.06, non-significant) and showed no dementia risk reduction.

**Cognitive flexibility training** shows emerging near transfer within paradigm. Wen et al. (2023) demonstrated abstract meta-learning about flexibility demands, but Hohl & Dolcos (2024) noted traditional task-switching training effects "do not generalize to other cognitive or daily life skills." No large-scale trials exist specifically for flexibility training.

**Decision speed/SAT training** has no standalone meta-analyses. Evidence must be inferred from the broader processing speed literature, as the ACTIVE trial's speed training inherently involved rapid visual decision-making under time pressure.

### Training parameters that maximize transfer

The evidence converges on specific parameter ranges. **Total dose**: ~10–15 hours of initial training captures most benefits; beyond ~20 hours, diminishing returns occur (Hardy et al., 2023; Belleville et al., 2022). **Frequency**: Lampit et al. (2014) found **≤3 sessions per week** optimal — training more than 3 times weekly was counterproductive. **Session duration**: 30–60 minutes appears optimal; under 30 minutes may be insufficient (Lampit et al., 2014). **Booster sessions** are critical: in the ACTIVE trial, speed training *without* boosters did not reach significance for dementia reduction, while *with* boosters it did (Coe et al., 2026). **Variability**: multi-domain training generally produces broader transfer than single-task training.

### Simons et al. (2016) remains the definitive review, largely reinforced

Simons et al. found "extensive evidence that brain-training interventions improve performance on the trained tasks, less evidence that such interventions improve performance on closely related tasks, and little evidence that training enhances performance on distantly related tasks." Effect sizes were "inversely related to the design quality of the experiment." These conclusions have been reinforced by Gobet & Sala (2023) and multiple null results for WM far transfer. The ACTIVE trial's 20-year follow-up provides the strongest counter-evidence for one specific type of training (speed-of-processing with boosters), which Simons et al. already acknowledged as a notable exception.

### The ACTIVE trial stands as the field's most important study

The ACTIVE trial (Ball et al., 2002; Rebok et al., 2014; Coe et al., 2026) tested three training modalities in 2,832 independently living older adults. Speed-of-processing training was the only modality that was **computerized and adaptive** — memory and reasoning training were instructor-led and strategy-based. This design difference may be crucial: adaptive difficulty, implicit/automatic learning, and progressive challenge characterize the speed training uniquely. The 20-year finding that boosted speed training reduced dementia risk by 25% was called "astonishing" and "the strongest evidence to date supporting the use of cognitive training" by independent experts. However, replication in independent trials is still needed, and several ACTIVE investigators have disclosed conflicts of interest (financial ties to Posit Science).

## SECTION 8: Claims must be precise, study-specific, and tier-appropriate

### Three tiers of increasingly risky language

**Level 1 — Measurement** (lowest risk): "This task measures your visual processing speed" or "Track your performance on attention tasks over time." These are descriptive and factual, requiring only reasonable psychometric support.

**Level 2 — Training improvement** (moderate risk): "Regular practice can improve your performance on this type of task" or "Users who trained showed improvement in speed of processing on similar tasks." The FTC requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence" for health benefit claims, generally meaning RCTs.

**Level 3 — Real-world transfer** (highest risk): Only defensible with strong RCT evidence for the specific product tested. "In the NIH-funded ACTIVE study, 10 hours of speed-of-processing training was associated with improved everyday speed" (with citation) is defensible for BrainHQ's Double Decision — but NOT for other apps. Claims like "improves performance at work, in school, and in athletics" or "delays dementia" without product-specific RCTs triggered the Lumosity settlement.

### The Lumosity settlement established clear boundaries

The FTC filed against Lumos Labs in January 2016, resulting in a **$50 million judgment** (suspended to $2 million paid) for deceptive advertising. The FTC challenged three categories of claims: (1) that Lumosity improved everyday performance, school/work outcomes, and athletics; (2) that it delayed or prevented dementia and Alzheimer's disease; (3) that it reduced cognitive impairment from stroke, TBI, PTSD, ADHD, and chemotherapy effects. The FTC also found 46 testimonials were solicited through undisclosed prize contests. FTC Director Jessica Rich stated: "Lumosity preyed on consumers' fears about age-related cognitive decline... Lumosity simply did not have the science to back up its ads."

### BrainHQ's evidence base is uniquely strong

BrainHQ (Posit Science) has **~300+ published peer-reviewed studies**, including 96 in healthy older adults and ~200 across clinical conditions. A 2017 systematic review in *Neuropsychological Review* found BrainHQ had the most studies (10 of 26 qualifying) and highest quality (8 of 10 rated highest) among 18 commercial brain-training products. Critically, BrainHQ contains the actual exercise (Double Decision) used in the ACTIVE trial, giving it the **only direct claim** to ACTIVE's results. Most studies are independently conducted and NIH-funded. BrainHQ uses precise, study-specific language and explicitly disclaims diagnostic or clinical treatment intent.

### The Stanford consensus debate remains unresolved

The **Stanford Center on Longevity consensus letter** (October 2014, 70+ signatories) stated "there is little evidence that playing brain games improves underlying broad cognitive abilities." The **counter-letter** (December 2014, 127+ signatories including Michael Merzenich) argued "a substantial and growing body of evidence shows that certain cognitive training regimens can significantly improve cognitive function." **Simons et al. (2016)** examined the evidence cited by both letters and found none of the cited studies conformed to all best practices — the truth lies between the two positions, with the specific intervention and evidence quality being decisive.

### Regulatory classification determines everything

The **FDA General Wellness Guidance (updated January 2026)** provides a narrow pathway for avoiding medical device classification. A product qualifies for enforcement discretion when it promotes healthy lifestyle (including "mental acuity") without disease-specific claims and is low-risk. The critical boundary: **"mental acuity" is wellness; "treating cognitive impairment" is medical.** Any reference to specific diseases (Alzheimer's, ADHD, dementia) in marketing — even app store descriptions — can trigger FDA medical device classification, requiring 510(k) or De Novo clearance. The FTC's 2022 Health Products Compliance Guidance removed the word "flexible" from the substantiation standard, reflecting stricter enforcement. International considerations include the UK ASA (which ruled against a brain training app implying ADHD diagnosis in 2024) and the EU Medical Device Regulation.

## Conclusion: what this evidence means for Pulse

The scientific landscape for cognitive training is more nuanced than either enthusiasts or skeptics suggest. **Speed-of-processing training is the only modality with robust evidence for lasting real-world transfer** — 20-year dementia risk reduction through adaptive, implicit, divided-attention paradigms. Working memory training reliably improves trained-task performance but has essentially no demonstrated far transfer. Cognitive flexibility training is theoretically well-grounded and benefits from the Wen et al. (2023) breakthrough showing abstract flexibility transfer, but lacks large-scale outcome trials. Decision speed is partially distinct and trainable via drift rate improvements, though the evidence base is thinner.

For Pulse's adaptive engine, targeting **~80% accuracy** using trial-by-trial staircase adaptation is well-supported, but the 85% rule should be treated as a useful starting heuristic rather than a universal law. Adaptive difficulty is clearly superior to fixed-easy training but offers less clear advantages over other forms of variable difficulty — the critical ingredient appears to be exposure to appropriate challenge variation rather than the algorithm itself.

The most actionable recommendations are: (1) prioritize adaptive choice RT paradigms over simple RT, drawing from the UFOV paradigm's success; (2) add inhibitory control and spatial reasoning as new dimensions; (3) never claim far transfer without product-specific RCT evidence; (4) position Pulse as "cognitive fitness" under FDA general wellness guidance, avoiding all disease-specific language; and (5) build booster session mechanics into the training architecture, given their critical role in long-term retention. The strongest honest claim Pulse can make today is that regular adaptive cognitive training improves performance on trained tasks and closely related skills — and that maintaining this cognitive engagement is associated with better cognitive health outcomes in the broader literature.
